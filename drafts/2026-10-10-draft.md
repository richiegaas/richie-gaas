**Introduction.** Guam calls itself "Where America’s Day Begins," but if a war with China ever comes, this island may be where it begins too. China’s DF-26 ballistic missile is nicknamed the “Guam Killer” or “Guam Express” because of its potential to strike the island, and China’s air force has released a video simulating a strike on a runway that closely resembles Andersen Air Force Base in Guam (Shaikh, 2018; Stimson Center, 2024; Tian & Blanchard, 2020). America’s answer is missile defense, a network of radars and interceptors, built to find and destroy incoming missiles in mid-flight. Because a single interceptor can cost anywhere from about $2 million to $28 million, the question is what mix of missile defense capabilities should be bought and deployed to Guam (Missile Defense Advocacy Alliance [MDAA], 2025).

**Background.** In September 2025, the Department of War approved a roughly $8 billion dollar, 360-degree shield called the Guam Defense System, or GDS (Arms Control Association, 2025). The plan is to field it in phases, with elements arriving in fiscal year 2027 and the last by fiscal year 2032 (U.S. Government Accountability Office [GAO], 2025). The House and Senate Appropriations Committees were faced with a framework like our cohort’s first economics problem set, the bed-farm model: sixteen war sites, three missile capabilities, and an $8 billion dollar cap. Table 1 introduces each interceptor, what it can stop, and what it costs to field.

**Thesis Statement.** Guam is currently protected by a Navy Aegis Weapon System and an Army Terminal High Altitude Area Air Defense (THAAD) battery. These systems have coverage against ballistic missiles like China’s DF-26, but limited coverage against other kinds of threats, and removing these capabilities and deploying them elsewhere runs into sunk costs. So, the next dollar should be invested in one missile system with interceptors that can get the most bang for the buck to defend the island. The Department of War wants to deploy an uncommitted amount of Patriot Missile Systems with an undisclosed number of Patriot Advanced Capability-3 Missile Segment Enhanced (PAC-3 MSE) interceptors. This missile capability covers the threat gap: Patriot can engage ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and aircraft (CSIS Missile Defense Project, 2023b). The suggestion is to deploy a total of 8 Patriot Missile Systems with 576 PAC-3 MSE interceptors, to adequately achieve holistic island defense for Guam. This would cost about $5.6 billion of the $8 billion allocated for GDS: eight batteries at roughly $0.4 billion each, plus 576 interceptors at about $4.2 million each (Table 1; Cancian & Karako, 2022; Norsk Luftvern, 2026). It also appears to be the direction the Department of War is heading, as a new Patriot battalion stands up on Guam in October 2026 (Trevithick & Altman, 2026).

To test this suggestion, I compared three options using the costs in Table 1, placeholder kill probabilities, three hypothetical raids, and a limit of eight Patriot batteries (Table 2). Eight Patriot batteries with 576 MSE interceptors cost about $5.6 billion and defeat 92 percent of a ballistic-heavy raid and 89 percent of a cruise-heavy raid, but only 49 percent of a 300-drone raid, because the model credits Patriot with no drone defense. The current Guam plan spends less, $3.1 to $4.1 billion, but scores lower in every raid, from 68 percent down to 43 percent. An optimal mix that leans on Patriot CRI and Aegis SM-6 scores highest, 97 percent down to 59 percent, but spends nearly the entire $8 billion. The Patriot-only buy is therefore a strong middle option, but how well it performs depends on the kind of raid China launches. However, unlike our cohort’s bed-farm model, this research discovers that there are macroeconomic implications of a missile system-war site model for Guam that affect other countries who rely on the same capability.

**A Fixed Supply.** The 2026 war with Iran consumed nearly two-thirds of the U.S. Patriot inventory. Analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimate that the United States has only 759 to 827 Patriot interceptors left, about a third of its pre-war stock (Korte, 2026). Every PAC-3 MSE interceptor comes off the same production line. In 2025, Lockheed Martin delivered 620 PAC-3 MSE interceptors while working at a capacity of roughly 600 per year. Only in January 2026 did the Department of War sign a seven-year framework to raise that capacity to about 2,000 per year (Lockheed Martin [LM], 2026). Measured against those numbers, Guam’s proposed 576 MSE interceptors equal 93 percent of an entire year of 2025 production and 70 to 76 percent of what the United States has left. In the short run, interceptor supply behaves like a nearly vertical supply curve. Factories cannot add production lines overnight, and a new buyer cannot call more missiles into existence, it can only raise their price.

**Crowding Out Allies.** When output is fixed, every interceptor the U.S. government buys for Guam is one that cannot go to an ally. Taiwan plans to spend about US$636.9 million on 102 PAC-3 MSE interceptors, and its officials warned that U.S. restocking after the Middle East conflict could delay delivery (“Taiwan Concerned Iran Conflict,” 2026). Guam’s order alone is more than five and a half times Taiwan’s. In Ukraine, some Patriot crews had exhausted their limited interceptor stocks by August 2026, and the country cannot manufacture PAC-3 interceptors domestically, so it depends on outside production (Servaes, 2026). U.S. forces in the Middle East have drawn heavily on the same stockpile (Cicurel, 2025; Korte, 2026). The opportunity cost of a PAC-3 parked on Guam is not measured only in dollars, it is a PAC-3 that is not defending Kyiv or Taipei. Taiwan sits on the front line of the same Chinese missile threat Guam is preparing for, so a Guam-first allocation that delays Taiwan’s defenses may weaken the very deterrence the Pacific theater depends on.

**Trade Effects.** Trade ties the Guam decision to the wider economy as well. Patriot sales to allies are U.S. exports, and diverting output to Guam lowers those exports while pushing allies toward substitutes. Viable options include NASAMS and the IRIS-T missile systems, which can handle less demanding threats but cannot fully replace Patriot against ballistic missiles (Servaes, 2026). Allied production offers a way out, grounded in comparative advantage. Japan, which builds PAC-3 missiles under license, completed its first export of those missiles to the United States in 2025 to replenish U.S. stocks strained by aid to Ukraine (“Japan Completes PAC-3,” 2025). Expanding co-production adds to global supply instead of dividing a fixed amount. Yet, the production side carries some risk too. In December 2024, China banned exports of gallium, germanium, and antimony to the United States, citing their military uses; these metals are vital inputs for defense technologies (Baskaran & Schwartz, 2024). Beijing suspended the ban in November 2025, but only until November 27, 2026 (“Beijing Lifts Restrictions,” 2025). That leverage remains in China’s hands, and it directly affects the U.S. defense posture in the Pacific.

**Recommendation.** The House and Senate appropriations committees should use their current Guam Defense System fielding schedule (GAO, 2025) to back-load Guam’s PAC-3 MSE purchases into FY2029 through FY2032, when the seven-year production ramp is moving toward 2,000 interceptors per year, rather than compete with Taiwan and Ukraine for today’s roughly 600 (LM, 2026). Part of the funding should go to Lockheed Martin’s production capacity itself, which shifts the supply curve outward instead of simply bidding up the price. This recommendation requires a strategy to acquire critical minerals from alternate sources to address the risk exposed by China’s export ban. With a fixed supply, putting Guam first raises the price for every buyer and weakens many global defense partners, to include allies who help deter China in the first place. Phasing the purchase still delivers Guam’s 576 interceptors by 2032, with less pressure on price and less damage to the allies the United States is counting on around the world.
