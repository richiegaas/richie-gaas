# Cost-exchange ratio — engagement brief

Question. Given the Guam Defense System's budget and interceptor production limits, what mix of interceptors defeats the most expected threats per taxpayer dollar across ballistic, cruise, air-breathing, and drone (UAS) attacks? And how should that mix change if China shifts the composition of its raids?
Who decides. The House and Senate defense appropriations subcommittees fund these programs line by line, advised by the Missile Defense Agency and INDOPACOM. Guam's non-voting delegate to Congress and the Government of Guam are the local advocates. The paper is written so a staffer on one of those committees could act on it.
The challenge. Guam's new missile defense is a roughly $8 billion system spread across 16 sites, approved in September 2025 (Arms Control Association). Its core is a land-based Aegis system firing SM-3 and SM-6 interceptors from Mk 41 launchers. The interceptors it can draw on differ in cost by orders of magnitude, and each covers only some threat types. With a fixed budget, every expensive interceptor bought is several cheaper ones not bought. Choosing the mix is an allocation problem, and it affects taxpayers, roughly 150,000 residents, and the bases at Andersen Air Force Base and Naval Base Guam.
Why now, not in general.
• The loadout is being bought now. GAO reports first deployment in FY2027 and final elements arriving by FY2032 (GAO-25-108187).
• The architecture is still in flux. Development of the AN/TPY-6 radar stopped in January 2025 (GAO), and planners cut the site count from 22 to 16 (Breaking Defense).
• The threat mix is shifting toward cheap cruise missiles and drones, which make expensive interceptors a poor trade. Analysts warn China could fire salvos an order of magnitude larger than Iran's April 2024 strike on Israel.
Working hypothesis (to be tested, not assumed; restate it in your own words). The budget-optimal loadout puts a smaller share of dollars into high-end ballistic interceptors than a threat-severity ranking would suggest, and more into cheaper layers against cruise missiles and drones. That's because the extra protection each dollar buys falls fastest for the most expensive interceptors. The recommended mix should be the one that holds up across raid scenarios, not the best one for a single guess.
How I'd know I was wrong.
• The optimal mix keeps a high SM-3 share in every raid scenario, which would mean ballistic threats dominate regardless of cost.
• Small changes in assumed kill probabilities flip the recommended mix, which would mean the paper should recommend a decision rule and a data request rather than a specific loadout.
• The non-interceptor benchmark (hardening) beats every interceptor at the margin, which would mean the real answer is outside the loadout question.
Scope.
• In: four threat classes covered by about five representative interceptor types; a fixed interceptor budget; annual production caps; three raid scenarios; one non-interceptor benchmark; unclassified cost data.
• Out: radar and command-and-control choices; classified performance data; nuclear deterrence; whether the U.S. should be in the western Pacific.
Deliverable. A research paper of about 3,000 words with at least one evidence-bearing figure, a recommendation with a named decision-maker, and an AI-use log. The reader is my MBA micro/macro economics professor, who grades it on the four jobs. The appropriations committees stay the decision-maker inside the paper. The model is an Excel and Solver build, like the marginal-analysis engagement, and the macro section keeps three channels: fiscal policy and opportunity cost, inelastic supply and defense inflation, and trade and input dependence.
