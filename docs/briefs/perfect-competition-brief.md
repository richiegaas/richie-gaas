# <Engagement> — engagement brief

## The problem
The problem is that I have a total of 64 beds of crops and I do not know what combination of Tomatoes, Carrots, and Mesclun I should plant and how many beds of each crop should I maintain in order to reap the most harvest, optimize efficiency, and reduce costs. I have $20,000 in fixed costs, the farmer gets paid about $50,000 per season where she spends 720 field hours and rakes in $34.72/hour, and I can have up to four temporary workers who can get paid maximum $25,000 each, billed at 1,440 hours of work time each worker, which is about a $17.36/hour wage rate. For Tomatoes, I can only have a maximum of 20 beds. Each tomato bed renders $8,800 in revenue, the cost of labor hours per bed is 2.50 per week, the cost of fertilizer for Tomatoes is $880, and the diminishing returns on the costs of labor is at a loss rate of 10% of labor hours spent per bed. For Carrots, I can only have a maximum of 20 beds. Each carrot bed renders $2,094 in revenue, the cost of labor hours per bed is 0.833 per week, the cost of fertilizer for Carrots is $440, and the diminishing returns on the costs of labor is at a loss rate of 2.50% of labor hours spent per bed. For Mesclun, I can only have a maximum of 30 beds. Each mesclun bed renders $2,700 in revenue, the cost of labor hours per bed is 1.25 per week, the cost of fertilizer for Mesclun is $880, and the diminishing returns on the costs of labor is at a loss rate of 1.25% of labor hours spent per bed. Here is the labor formula for reference: q x hours-per-bed-per-week x 36 x (1 + rate) to the power of q.

## What I am assuming
One assumption is that I will be able to yield all vegetables that I harvested, there will not be a bed of vegetables that spoils or that I cannot harvest. Second assumption is that pest pressure, harvest bottlenecks, and walking time do not affect the ability to reap 100% of all crops I planted in one season. Third assumption is that the farmers' market sets the prices of these crops, not me. Fourth assumption is that myself and four temporary workers are all that's needed to successfully and efficiently plant these crops and harvest.

## Hypothesis
"I expect 20 carrot beds, 30 mesclun beds, and 14 tomato beds, because this would maximize efficiency in terms of optimizing cost and wages, and because because tomatoes earn about four times per bed and I doubt the labor penalty closes that gap."

## How I would know I was wrong
If the model plants less than 25 mesclun beds, the labor penalty for mesclun beds is stronger than I assumed. 
If the model plants more than 17 tomato beds, the labor penalty for tomato beds is weaker than I assumed. 
If the carrots come back below 20 beds, then something stopped them before their cap and I do not know what.
If there are less mesclun beds than there are carrot and tomato beds, then the labor penalty of both tomato and carrot beds combined are weaker than I assumed and mesclun beds have a higher labor penalty than I first imagined.
